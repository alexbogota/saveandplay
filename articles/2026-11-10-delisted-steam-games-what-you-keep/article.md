Every so often a game "will be delisted soon". On November 10, 2026, gg.deals wrote about a free-to-play title that will leave Steam but, in its words, can still be played "as long as it's in your library". That is a useful reminder: a store page can disappear while your copy stays. It is also a reminder to ask what exactly you keep.

This guide uses two Valve pages: the developer page on retiring a game and the Steam Subscriber Agreement. Neither is written for buyers in the first place, so we say where they stop.

## The short version

- **Owners keep the game.** Valve's developer page says "Retired games will remain in players' Steam libraries" and that players "will continue to be able to download, install, and launch the game."
- **New sales stop.** Valve hides the purchase option, and selling new Steam keys is prohibited once the game is off the store.
- **Online features are the weak spot.** The same page treats hosted servers as a special case, so a game that needs servers may not work in full.
- **The agreement does not promise access forever.** It says content is "licensed, not sold", and we found no line that promises it will stay available indefinitely.

## What Valve tells developers

The page "Removing a product from Steam" describes how a developer retires a game: when they cancel it, or "have lost distribution rights and need to stop selling a title". Once Valve is contacted, it will "hide the purchase option". The store page stays up, but the game is removed from store listings.

For people who already own the game, the page is plain: it stays in the library, and you can download, install and launch it. Purchases fall under the normal refund policy, though Valve "may offer extended refunds in some cases".

## Where the promise gets thin

**Servers.** Valve asks developers whether the game relies on "servers or other functions that customers will no longer be able to access", and says to explain what to expect "especially if your game relies on hosted servers that customers will no longer be able to access." So "I can launch it" and "everything still works" are different claims. Multiplayer, online-only and live-service parts depend on the developer.

**The agreement.** Section 2.A of the Steam Subscriber Agreement says: "The Content and Services are licensed, not sold." It also says the license ends when the agreement or the subscription ends, and section 7.C says Valve does not guarantee "continuous, error-free, virus-free or secure operation". The agreement does not say that a given game will never be removed. We read it as: delisting by itself is not the end of your license, but the page where you bought it is not a promise.

**Free-to-play.** For free-to-play games Valve asks developers to give at least 90 days' notice and to turn off in-game purchases first. That is a developer rule, not something the buyer can rely on to the day.

## Keys and delisted games

Valve's page says developers who sold keys elsewhere must retire the game in those stores too, get a list of unsold keys from each retailer and ban them. For you this means a key from a reseller for a game that has left Steam is a risk you should price in. Our guide to [buying a Steam key from a reseller](/guides/steam-key-reseller-risks/) lists the checks.

## Five checks before you buy a game that may leave

1. **Does it need servers to play?** If yes, expect the developer to decide how long that lasts.
2. **Is there a "delisting" or "end of sale" notice on the page or in news?** Read it for what stays working.
3. **Is it a key from another store?** Be careful with keys for games that are off Steam.
4. **Is it still inside the refund window?** The normal rule is two weeks and under two hours of play. See [how Steam refunds work](/guides/how-steam-refunds-work/).
5. **Would you be fine if the game stayed in your library but online features went?** If not, wait.
