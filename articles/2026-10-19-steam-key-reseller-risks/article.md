A Steam key is a code. You buy it in some store, paste it into Steam, and the game lands in your library. Many stores sell keys legitimately, and some marketplaces let independent sellers list keys at lower prices. The price gap can be real. So can the trouble if something goes wrong.

The key point is simple: once you pay somewhere other than Steam, Steam is no longer the one who answers your questions. Here is what Steam's own pages say about that, and a short check to run before you pay.

## The short version

- **Valve does not refund keys bought elsewhere.** Its refund page says "Valve cannot provide refunds for purchases made outside of Steam."
- **The seller is your first contact.** The Steam Subscriber Agreement tells you to direct questions about a key to the reseller you bought it from.
- **Not every key is meant to be sold.** Valve's rules for developers say some key types must never be sold. A buyer cannot see the key type, so the seller's reputation matters.
- **Cheaper is not the same as safe.** Check the seller, the region and the refund terms, not only the price.

## What Steam covers, and what it does not

On the Steam store, the refund offer is "within two weeks of purchase and with less than two hours of playtime". Our [guide to Steam refunds](/guides/how-steam-refunds-work/) explains where that rule stops. The same refund page lists "CD keys or Steam wallet cards purchased from third parties" as purchases Valve cannot refund.

In the Steam Subscriber Agreement, section 3.F says that for a key bought from an authorized reseller, "you agree to direct all questions regarding the Product Key to that reseller." In plain words: a dead code, a wrong region or a charge you did not expect is a conversation with the store or the seller, not with Steam.

## How a key can go wrong

These are the cases that follow from the rules we read. We are not saying they are common. We have no data on how often they happen.

- **The key was not sold properly.** Valve's key documentation says developers may request keys only for content on Steam, and that "Default Release" keys are "appropriate for retail boxes or sales on other digital stores." It also says "It is never OK to sell Release-State Override keys," that Dev Comp keys "should never be distributed to customers," and that Playtest keys should never be sold. Valve also describes fraudsters "impersonating journalists or storefronts to acquire keys, and then reselling those keys for profit."
- **The key can be revoked.** The same document tells developers they "may ban keys to revoke access from users whose license has expired," and lists revoking "Keys that have been refunded" among common reasons. It adds: "You should never ban or disable Steam keys that have been legitimately sold to customers." A key that was refunded to the original buyer is the kind that can stop working later.
- **The region does not match.** Section 3.A of the Subscriber Agreement says you agree not to use "IP proxying or other methods to disguise the place of your residence", and that Valve may terminate your access to your Account if you do. Buying a key meant for another country and hiding your location to use it is a risk to your whole account, not just to the key. Our [guide to regional prices](/guides/why-game-prices-differ-by-country/) explains why prices differ.
- **You cannot undo the activation.** Section 9.B says the original activation key "will not be able to be registered to any other account." Once the code is in your library, it is yours to keep, and it is not yours to pass on.
- **The account can be restricted.** Section 9.C says Valve may restrict or cancel an Account or a Subscription if you break the agreement, and that no refund is given in that case. We are not saying a resold key leads to this. We are saying that if something goes wrong with a key, the agreement does not promise you a remedy from Valve.

## A five-minute check before you pay

1. **Who is the seller?** A store selling its own stock is different from a marketplace where independent sellers list keys. Find the page that says which one you are buying from.
2. **What are the refund and claim terms?** Look for the store's own page. How many days do you have to report a bad key? Who pays if it fails?
3. **Is the region stated?** The listing should say where the key works. If it does not, do not guess.
4. **Is the edition clear?** Standard, Deluxe, DLC included or not. The listing should match the Steam page.
5. **Is the price believable?** Compare it to the current Steam price and the price history. A huge gap deserves one more look. Our [guide to real and fake discounts](/guides/real-vs-fake-game-discounts/) has the steps.
6. **Redeem early.** Paste the key into Steam soon after buying, so that a problem shows up while the seller's terms still allow a claim.

## When a key is a reasonable choice

- The store is a known one, the seller terms are written down, and the region is stated.
- The saving is large enough to matter to you, and you accept that the refund button is not on your side.
- You are not tempted to hide your location to make a key work.

If one of these is missing, buying on Steam or a DRM-free store costs more, but the refund rules are written down on one page.
